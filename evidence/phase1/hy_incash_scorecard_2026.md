# Nigeria’s 2026 Startup Scorecard, Who Won, Who Folded, and the Lessons Everyone Should Steal

On paper, 2026 is a great year for Nigerian startups. They raised $635 million between January and September, up 157% from the same period last year and the highest nine month total since 2022. Nigeria is back at the top in Africa.

Look at the shutdown notices, though, and it feels like a different industry. Uber left. Moove left. Klump, Gigbanc, Chimoney and GoLemon closed or stopped trading. Moniepoint is reshaping its engineering teams.

Both stories are true, and the gap between them is the real lesson of the year. Here is who won, who failed, and what it all teaches.

## The Headline Number, And Its Catch

Nigeria’s 635 million figure is real,but it is lopsided. Four deals made up most of it:Moove(250 million), Flutterwave (100million),Jumia(50 million) and Yellow Card ($40 million). H1 funding was about $214 million, which means the third quarter alone brought in roughly $420 million, largely because of Moove’s August round.

And Moove has since closed its Nigerian business. So the biggest cheque of the year went to a company that left home.

Read the number as a sign that investors still back proven, later stage companies with big ambitions, not that money is flowing freely to every founder with a pitch deck.

## Who Won

**Flutterwave: from payments to banking.** It bought open banking startup Mono in January and acquired a CBN microfinance bank licence, reported in April, which lets it hold deposits directly and offer business accounts, payroll, payouts and working capital without third party banks. TechCabal’s headline said it had crossed $40 billion in lifetime payments. It also raised $100 million this year. Winning move: own more of the stack.

**Terra Industries: deeptech with serious backers.** The drone and autonomous security maker topped Nairametrics’ list of best funded startups by May, with $33.8 million across a seed round and a February round, backed by investors including 8VC, Valor Equity Partners and Lux Capital.

**MAX: mobility with a financing engine.** The logistics and mobility startup raised $24 million, half equity and half debt, showing that asset heavy businesses can attract capital when the financing structure is right.

**Stablecoin and digital asset builders.** Checker raised $8 million for stablecoin powered financial services, OneDosh raised $3 million for stablecoin payments, and Yellow Card raised $40 million. Then the CBN set a ₦15 billion capital requirement for foreign currency stablecoin issuers, showing how fast regulation is catching up. The winners here will be those who can afford to be properly licensed.

**Vatar and Lagos Life: the surprise breakout.** A browser game built in about a night reached millions of players and closed a $500,000 angel round at a reported $10 million valuation, with backing from fintech founders. It is early, and it is also proof that small teams with local insight can still break out.

**Moove: a global win, a local exit.** Its $2.1 billion valuation made it a unicorn, and it is giving drivers about N35 billion in cars on the way out. It is a win for the company and a warning about what happens when a business depends on one platform.

## Who Failed, And Why

**Klump, after five years.** The BNPL startup raised only about $1.2 million, announced its closure on October 25, and its founders pointed to trouble raising debt and slow lender integrations. After failing to raise the lending capital it needed, it stopped holding its own loan book and became a marketplace of lenders, but partnerships reportedly took 6 to 18 months to go live and some nearly three years. They received one acquisition offer this year and declined it. Their own lesson: BNPL works better as part of a bigger ecosystem than on its own. A caution, though: that account comes from the founders’ own note, and competitors such as CredPal and Easybuy remain active.

**Gigbanc.** It wound down in July after struggling to secure enough funding, despite processing more than N10 billion in transactions. Volume without capital is not a business.

**Chimoney.** The cross border payments startup halted new transactions from May 1 after failing to raise enough capital and was later acquired.

**Baxi.** Onafriq closed this agent banking business in October, while keeping its other lines. The pressures cited include inflation, naira depreciation, rising costs and the shift to instant account to account transfers.

**GoLemon.** It shut down in July with 33 jobs affected.

**Uber and Moove.** Two giants of Nigerian mobility left within weeks of each other, with dollar costs, naira earnings, fuel and vehicle costs, and competition from Bolt and inDrive all cited.

## The Pattern Behind the Failures

Look closely and a handful of causes keep repeating.

**1\. Capital, not customers, was the bottleneck.** Gigbanc had volume. Klump had distribution through major marketplaces. Neither had the capital or control they needed.

**2\. Dependence on someone else’s pace.** Klump relied on lenders who set their own limits and speed. Moove relied on Uber. When the partner moved, the business stopped.

**3\. Currency mismatch.** Many firms earn in naira and pay for some costs in dollars. Every depreciation squeezes margins.

**4\. Thin margins in a price sensitive market.** Rising energy, compliance and operating costs leave little room for error.

**5\. Growth built for easier funding years.** Teams and cost structures designed for abundant capital are now being reshaped, as Moniepoint’s restructuring shows.

## The Lessons

**Own your critical dependency, or have a plan B.** The founders of Klump summed up two lessons that pull in opposite directions: operate inside a bigger ecosystem, and own your capital and loan book. The honest takeaway is that you need either control of the thing that sets your pace or several partners so none can stall you.

**Earn in the currency you spend in.** Match revenue and costs where possible, and price in ways that survive depreciation.

**Licences are becoming moats.** Flutterwave’s banking licence and the CBN’s new stablecoin capital rules both point the same way: regulatory readiness is turning into a competitive advantage.

**Real world businesses can raise money.** Terra, MAX and Beacon Power Services show that hardware, logistics and energy startups attract capital when the model is clear.

**Smaller can survive.** Klump survived five years on about $1.2 million. Companies that stretch capital carefully last longer than those that burn it.

**Don’t read the headline number.** A record total hid a brutal year for smaller startups. Watch how many companies are raising, not just how much.

## The Bottom Line

2026 is Nigeria’s startup scene growing up. The winners are bigger, more licensed, more vertically integrated and more selective about partners. The casualties are mostly smaller companies that depended on capital, partners or conditions they could not control.

For founders, the message is simple: build something that survives losing your biggest partner. The funding will keep coming for those who do.
