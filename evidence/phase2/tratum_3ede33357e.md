**Nigerian startup Tratum is developing hardware and embedded financial infrastructure that allows banks and other regulated institutions to extend their services into places where connectivity is unreliable.**

Built by YHWH Vision Ventures Technologies Ltd., which was formally incorporated in 2025, [Tratum](https://www.tratum-website.vercel.app/) traces its origins to Nigeria’s 2023 cash scarcity crisis, when millions of people had money in their bank accounts but struggled to access or use it.

“If money is increasingly digital, why should access to that money completely depend on a functioning network, a smartphone, a remote server or a particular digital channel at the exact moment someone needs to transact?” said Segun Ademigoke, the startup’s founder and CEO.

At the heart of the ecosystem is the Tratum Node, a secure physical financial endpoint that combines secure hardware, cryptographic device identity, local transaction state and communication capabilities. Under rules set by the institution, a Node can carry out approved transactions locally, record them cryptographically, and then synchronise and reconcile with the bank’s systems once connectivity returns.

“We are not trying to become the bank, replace the central bank or compete with consumer-facing financial institutions. We are building technology that those institutions can use to extend what they already do,” Ademigoke said.

He said the startup is targeting commercial banks, central banks and monetary authorities, with potential applications in digital currency infrastructure and monetary distribution, as well as the merchants and individuals who ultimately use financial services.

Tratum faces competition from several parts of the financial technology stack, including card and POS networks, mobile money platforms, infrastructure providers such as FIS and Fiserv, and companies building offline payment and CBDC technology.

“Our competitive position is not simply “we work offline”. That would be too narrow,” said Ademigoke. “We are building a banking infrastructure layer for the physical economy.”

The startup has so far been funded through founder and family investment, along with government-assisted loan programmes. It has built and field-tested its devices, demonstrated the technology to financial institutions, and is now working towards integrating its first commercial bank into the Tratum network.

“We are not claiming millions of users or large transaction volumes yet. That would be premature,” Ademigoke said. “What we have demonstrated is that institutions are willing to engage with the technology.”

Tratum is pre-launch and pre-revenue. It plans to make money from Node hardware sales, recurring infrastructure and software fees, and transaction and settlement revenue, alongside bank integrations, APIs and licensing. Its internal model targets a selling price of around US$30 per Node and US$150 in annual recurring revenue per active Node.

The startup is currently focused on Nigeria, but its architecture is multi-currency, and it plans to expand into other African markets as regulatory approvals and institutional partnerships fall into place.

Ademigoke said the biggest challenge has been financing the move from validated technology to physical deployment, which requires funding for manufacturing, certification, inventory and integration. Tratum is now raising capital for that phase and is particularly keen to work with African investors.

“We believe Africa should not only be a market for financial technology built elsewhere. Africa can build the infrastructure itself,” he said.
